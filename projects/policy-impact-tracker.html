Research case study · Kenya · 2026

Agricultural policy · programme evidence · impact assessment

Are agricultural interventions delivering meaningful outcomes?

Building a structured evidence tracker to examine what public information can—and cannot—tell us about agricultural programme implementation, outcomes and impact in Kenya.

Scope10 interventions
Method18-step assessment framework
StatusPreliminary evidence assessments
ToolsExcel · desk research · evidence synthesis

Important: This is an independent research portfolio project, not an official government or donor evaluation. The ten intervention assessments are preliminary; they are not ten completed causal impact evaluations.

01 / The research problem

Implementation progress is not the same as demonstrated impact.

Agricultural programmes may report beneficiaries reached, inputs distributed, technologies adopted, infrastructure delivered or funds disbursed. Those indicators help describe implementation, but they do not automatically show whether farmers' productivity, income, food security or resilience improved because of the intervention.

The tracker was designed to organise the available evidence and make the gaps visible. It asks what is documented, how strong that evidence is, what outcomes can reasonably be described and what additional data would be needed for a credible impact assessment.

02 / Research question and objectives

What can public evidence establish about agricultural interventions?

  • Define each intervention and reconstruct its programme logic.
  • Identify intended outcomes, indicators and evidence sources.
  • Assess the availability and quality of implementation and outcome information.
  • Distinguish reported programme results from independently observed outcomes and causal estimates.
  • Document data gaps and specify the information needed for stronger evaluation.

The tracker considers five outcome dimensions: agricultural productivity, farmer livelihoods, market outcomes, food security and resilience, and sustainability. Equity, efficiency/value for money, data quality, attribution and limitations are also considered.

04 / Methodology

A repeatable 18-step evidence workflow.

  1. Define the intervention and reconstruct its policy or programme logic.
  2. Set evaluation questions, define indicators and map required data.
  3. Find or acquire relevant data, then assess data quality.
  4. Clean and standardise available data and integrate compatible datasets.
  5. Analyse implementation and outcomes separately.
  6. Assess attribution or impact only where the design and data support it.
  7. Consider equity, efficiency and value for money.
  8. Document limitations, prepare intervention findings and update the tracker.
  9. Archive reusable methodology and evidence records.

The detailed framework breaks these stages into 18 documented steps. It uses an evidence hierarchy that prioritises anonymised administrative records and original survey microdata, followed by methodologically transparent evaluations, official implementation reports, contextual statistics and secondary commentary.

Claims are labelled as reported, descriptive, attributable or not assessable. “Not assessable” means the required evidence is unavailable; it does not mean the intervention had no effect.

05 / Preliminary findings

Public documentation can describe implementation—but often cannot establish causality.

What the tracker can currently show All ten interventions have sufficient public documentation to define the intervention, reconstruct programme logic, record core indicators and describe reported implementation or monitoring progress.

What remains unresolved For most interventions, public information is not adequate for a credible causal estimate across the five outcome dimensions. Aggregate reports cannot isolate programme effects from weather, market conditions, other policies, participant selection or wider economic change.

Further review needed SDCP and UTaNRMP have publicly described comparison-based assessments, while NARIGP's completion report cites a beneficiary/non-beneficiary survey. Their technical methods, sampling, comparison construction, diagnostics and uncertainty require examination before causal conclusions are adopted.

Value for money is also not assessable from aggregate disbursement figures alone. It requires sufficiently detailed expenditure information, defensible unit-cost measures and clearly defined results.

06 / Data gaps and limitations

The next step is better data, not stronger claims.

  • Public sources are largely aggregate and do not consistently identify beneficiaries over time.
  • Reports may contain preliminary, revised or deduplicated monitoring figures.
  • Questionnaires, sampling frames, weights and raw survey data are often unavailable publicly.
  • Expenditure and procurement information needed for value-for-money analysis is not available in the required detail.
  • Pre/post change or reported indicator values are not causal estimates without a credible counterfactual.

Priority data needs include anonymised beneficiary and service registers, survey microdata, expenditure records, technical evaluation reports and comparable non-beneficiary or comparison-area data. Any future analysis would begin with data-quality checks and choose a causal method only if the available data support it.

07 / Outputs and reproducibility

A reusable evidence system, not just a collection of summaries.

  • Reusable 18-step assessment framework.
  • Ten intervention-level preliminary summaries.
  • Integrated master Excel workbook covering INT-01 to INT-10.
  • Evidence hierarchy, limitations and next-step documentation.
  • Public-source documentation and a data-acquisition plan.

08 / Research contribution

What this project demonstrates.

This project demonstrates applied agricultural policy research, structured desk review, indicator and evidence mapping, data-gap identification, research documentation and disciplined interpretation of programme results. It also shows how an agricultural economics perspective can connect programme implementation with farmer livelihoods, markets, food security, resilience and sustainability.

The tracker should not be used to rank institutions or make funding decisions without validation from implementing agencies and access to underlying evidence.
